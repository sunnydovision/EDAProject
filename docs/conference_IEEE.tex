\begin{abstract}
Human evaluation remains the dominant way to assess automated exploratory data analysis (AutoEDA) systems, but it is expensive, subjective, and difficult to reproduce. We introduce a reproducible automatic diagnostic framework for evaluating AutoEDA without human raters. The framework uses 9 metrics covering correctness, structural validity, statistical quality, subspace exploration, and question-insight alignment. We apply it to three representative pipelines: a QUIS-style structured question-guided system, ONLYSTATS as a statistics-based ablation, and a free-form LLM-based agentic baseline. Across Adidas US Sales, IBM Employee Attrition, and Online Sales, the framework shows that structured intermediate representations reduce column-type errors and support broader subspace exploration. The QUIS-style pipeline achieves 94.0\% average structural validity, compared with 40.0\% for the LLM baseline, and discovers more subspace insights on average (84.4\% vs. 37.4\%). It also attains the highest Score Uplift ($x$=1.067) and Simpson's Paradox Rate (30.1\%). Overall, the case study shows that schema-aware, question-guided representations improve the reliability of AutoEDA outputs and the breadth of subgroup discovery. More broadly, the paper contributes a reusable diagnostic framework for reproducible comparison of AutoEDA systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Automated exploratory data analysis (AutoEDA) systems are increasingly used to surface patterns, anomalies, and candidate hypotheses from unfamiliar datasets. As these systems become more capable, evaluating them becomes harder. Many papers still rely on human ratings or user studies, but such evaluations are expensive, subjective, and difficult to reproduce.

This challenge is especially visible in LLM-based AutoEDA pipelines. These systems can generate plausible insights even when the underlying analysis is structurally flawed, for example by using an invalid breakdown column, mismatching a pattern type to the data semantics, or missing informative subspaces. Human judgments alone do not reliably expose such failures, which motivates automatic diagnostic metrics that assess not only fluency, but also structural and statistical quality.

We introduce a reproducible automatic diagnostic framework for evaluating AutoEDA systems. It uses 9 metrics spanning correctness, structural validity, statistical quality, subspace exploration, and question-insight alignment. We validate the framework by applying it to three representative pipelines: a QUIS-style structured question-guided system, ONLYSTATS as a statistics-based ablation, and a free-form LLM-based agentic baseline. This setup demonstrates how the framework reveals systematic differences in structural validity, exploration breadth, and subspace quality.

We apply the framework across three datasets (Adidas US Sales, IBM Employee Attrition, and Online Sales) to answer two questions: Can automatic diagnostics support meaningful comparison of AutoEDA systems without human raters? And what do they reveal about the design principles underlying structured versus free-form approaches?

The answer is consistent across our case study. Structured intermediate representations improve structural validity and support broader subspace exploration. The QUIS-style pipeline achieves much higher structural validity than the free-form LLM baseline and more consistently discovers informative subgroup patterns. We do not claim that automatic metrics replace human evaluation in all settings; instead, they provide a reproducible diagnostic layer for studying failure modes and system differences.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item We introduce a reproducible automatic diagnostic framework with 9 metrics covering correctness, structural validity, statistical quality, subspace exploration, and question-insight alignment---enabling systematic comparison of AutoEDA systems without human raters.
\item We demonstrate through cross-dataset validation that structured intermediate representations substantially improve structural validity (94.0\% vs 40.0\%) and enable broader, higher-quality subspace exploration (84.4\% vs 37.4\% Subspace Rate, $x$=1.067 Score Uplift, with significant paradoxes discovered in 2 of 3 datasets).
\item Framework diagnostics establish that structural validity is a prerequisite for meaningful subspace analysis: systems with invalid breakdowns cannot produce statistically significant paradoxes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Automated EDA and Insight Evaluation}

Early automated EDA systems depend on statistical deviation to rank interesting findings. SEEDB~\cite{seedb} suggests visualizations by comparing target and reference distributions. QuickInsights~\cite{quickinsights} scores trends, outliers, and attributions by combined impact and significance. Zenvisage~\cite{zenvisage} allows users retrieve views matching specified visual patterns. However, these systems do not check whether insights have the correct structural form.

Visualization-focused systems evaluate quality through perceptual and organizational criteria. Voyager~\cite{voyager} ranks views using expressiveness and effectiveness rules. Voder~\cite{voder} combines natural language facts together with interactive visuals to surface findings during exploration. MetaInsight~\cite{metainsight} shapes insights into organized knowledge from data patterns. None of these check whether breakdown column types match the intended pattern.

More recent works introduce LLMs into EDA. InsightPilot~\cite{insightpilot} uses LLMs to suggest analysis actions, and be evaluated through user studies. QUIS~\cite{quis} generates guiding questions before insight search, but relies on human raters for evaluation---an approach that is costly and hard to reproduce.

\subsection{Subgroup Analysis and Pattern Quality}

Recent works do not only explore global statistics but also study conditional insights. Subgroup discovery~\cite{bach} formalizes finding data subsets behavior using measures like weighted relative accuracy---which we adapt as Score Uplift to measure how much stronger subspace patterns are compared to global ones.

Simpson's Paradox~\cite{simpson} brings up cases where a global association reverses direction within subgroups. Teng and Lin~\cite{teng} propose a kernel-based tree algorithm to detect and explain these in observational data. No prior work combines subgroup significance testing with paradox detection in a single EDA evaluation framework.
\subsection{Research Gap}

No existing AutoEDA evaluation framework checks structural validity---if the breakdown column type matches the intended pattern---or combines subgroup significance testing with paradox detection. \textbf{Our work} addresses both gaps with a fully automatic, human-free framework of 9 metrics covering correctness, structural validity, statistical quality, subspace exploration, and question quality. Our evaluation shows that LLM baselines have high systematic structural errors and produce zero significant paradoxes, while structured approaches achieve the highest SVR and discover meaningful pattern reversals.

\section{System Overview}

We compare three approaches to isolate (a) structured versus free-form reasoning, and (b) LLM-based versus statistical selection.

\subsection{QUIS-Style Structured Pipeline}

This pipeline uses schema-aware question generation (QUGEN) to create Insight Cards with explicit column-type constraints, then searches for matching insights (ISGEN). Each card specifies a breakdown column (B) and measure (M); each insight is a 4-tuple \textbf{(B, M, S, P)} with subspace filter S and pattern type P (TREND, OUTSTANDING\_VALUE, ATTRIBUTION, or DISTRIBUTION\_DIFFERENCE). ISGEN computes global patterns, then beam-searches for stronger subspace patterns using pattern-specific scoring (Mann-Kendall $\tau$ for TREND, attribution share for ATTRIBUTION, Jensen-Shannon divergence for DISTRIBUTION\_DIFFERENCE). Filter columns are selected randomly or via LLM suggestion (probability 0.5). This search algorithm is shared with ONLYSTATS to isolate the contribution of question generation.

\subsection{ONLYSTATS (Ablation Baseline)}

This ablation replaces QUGEN with statistical selection: computing Kruskal-Wallis scores~\cite{kruskal} for all (B,M) pairs and selecting top-20 per breakdown. Cards are generated via templates without LLM reasoning. ONLYSTATS uses the same ISGEN search, isolating whether LLM-generated questions improve exploration beyond statistical selection.

\subsection{LLM Agentic Baseline}

This free-form pipeline uses five sequential agents (data profiling, quality analysis, statistical analysis, pattern discovery, insight extraction) without structured representations. We implemented it using \texttt{gpt-5.4}. The LLM reasons freely over pre-computed evidence. We parse outputs into (B, M, S, P) format to test whether prompt-based reasoning achieves comparable validity to programmatic constraints.

\section{Evaluation Framework}

\subsection{Selected Metrics}

We selected 9 essential metrics from a larger list of candidates. We group them as follows.

\subsubsection{Group 1: Correctness}

\textit{Faithfulness} checks whether the numbers in an insight can be recomputed exactly from the dataset. For example, an insight reports a total sales value for one region, we recompute the aggregation from the CSV file to check the precision of the insight report. This metric works as a sanity check: a system with low faithfulness is hallucinating, making all other metrics unreliable. A high score is expected for good systems and confirms that the following metrics measure real patterns.

The formula is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Faithfulness} = \frac{|\{i \in I : \text{verified}(i)\}|}{|I|}
\end{equation}

where $I$ is the set of all insights, and $\text{verified}(i)$ returns true if for all reported values $v_r$ in insight $i$, the recomputed value $v_c$ satisfies $|v_r - v_c| < \epsilon$ with $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$.

\subsubsection{Group 2: Structural Quality}

\textit{Structural Validity Rate (SVR)} checks whether the breakdown column type matches the pattern type. For example, a TREND insight should use a date or time column as the breakdown. An ATTRIBUTION insight should use a categorical breakdown, not a numeric measure column.

The formula is:
\begin{equation}
\text{SVR} = \frac{|\{i \in I : \text{valid\_breakdown}(i)\}|}{|I|}
\end{equation}

where $\text{valid\_breakdown}(i)$ checks pattern-specific rules: for TREND, the breakdown $B_i$ must be temporal; for ATTRIBUTION and DISTRIBUTION\_DIFFERENCE, $B_i$ must be categorical or ID-type; for OUTSTANDING\_VALUE, any breakdown type is valid. We report SVR overall and also by pattern type. This also ensures that breakdown-measure pairs are actionable for subgroup analysis.

\textit{Pattern Coverage} counts how many out of the four patterns appear with a valid structure. A system that only produces one or two patterns is not doing broad EDA.

The formula is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Pattern\_Coverage} = \frac{|\{p \in P : \exists i \in I, \text{type}(i) = p \land \text{valid}(i)\}|}{|P|}
\end{equation}

where $P = \{\text{TR, OV, AT, DD}\}$ are the four pattern types (TREND, OUTSTANDING\_VALUE, ATTRIBUTION, DISTRIBUTION\_DIFFERENCE), and $\text{valid}(i)$ means the insight passes SVR check.

\subsubsection{Group 3: Statistical Quality}

\textit{Statistical Significance} checks the percentage of insights that pass a statistical test at p < 0.05. We use pattern-specific tests: Mann-Kendall for trends \cite{mann}, z-test for outstanding values, Chi-square test for attribution \cite{mchugh}, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for distribution differences \cite{kolmogorov}. Effect sizes are measured using Kendall's $\tau$ for trends \cite{kendall}, z/(z+1) for outstanding values, Cramér's V for attribution \cite{cramer}, and KS statistic for distribution differences.

The formula is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Statistical\_Significance} = \frac{|\{i \in I : p\text{-value}(i) < 0.05\}|}{|I|}
\end{equation}

where $p\text{-value}(i)$ is computed using the pattern-specific test for insight $i$. Only structurally valid insights (SVR = 1) are included in this calculation.

\subsubsection{Group 4: Exploration Quality}

\textit{Subspace Rate} measures how many insights contain at least one subspace filter. A high value means the system does not only provide global insights but also explores conditional cases, such as ``within Region = West''.

The formula is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Subspace\_Rate} = \frac{|\{i \in I : |S_i| > 0\}|}{|I|}
\end{equation}

where $S_i$ is the set of subspace filters for insight $i$. Each filter is a (column, value) pair that restricts the data to a subset.

\textit{Score Uplift from Subspace} measures whether subspace insights have stronger patterns than global insights. We define two related quantities. The absolute mean score of subspace insights is:
\begin{equation}
x = \frac{1}{|I_S|}\sum_{i \in I_S} \text{score}(i)
\end{equation}
and the uplift delta relative to global insights is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta = \frac{1}{|I_S|}\sum_{i \in I_S} \text{score}(i) - \frac{1}{|I_G|}\sum_{i \in I_G} \text{score}(i)
\end{equation}
where $I_S = \{i \in I : |S_i| > 0\}$, $I_G = \{i \in I : |S_i| = 0\}$, and $\text{score}(i)$ is the pattern strength (Kendall's $\tau$ for TREND, effect size otherwise). We report $x$ in all tables since $|I_G|$ can be zero for some systems; $\Delta$ is reported per-dataset in supplementary results.

\textit{Simpson's Paradox Rate (SPR)} detects statistically significant pattern reversals between global and subspace insights \cite{simpson}. A reversal occurs when the pattern direction changes---for example, a positive trend globally becomes negative in a subspace. We use pattern-specific tests (Mann-Kendall for TREND, Chi-square contingency table for ATTRIBUTION, KS test for DISTRIBUTION\_DIFFERENCE) and require both global and subspace patterns to be statistically significant (p < 0.05).

The formula is:
\begin{equation}
\text{SPR} = \frac{|\{i \in I_S : \text{is\_paradox}(i) \land \text{is\_significant}(i)\}|}{|I_S|}
\end{equation}

where $\text{is\_paradox}(i)$ detects pattern reversal and $\text{is\_significant}(i)$ requires p < 0.05 for both global and subspace patterns. Note that SPR is computed over subspace insights only ($I_S$), so a system with fewer subspace insights has fewer opportunities to produce paradoxes; Subspace Rate should be read alongside SPR.

\subsubsection{Group 5: Alignment and Coherence}

\textit{Question-Insight Alignment} measures how close the question text is to the final insight text. We compute cosine similarity using sentence embeddings. For example, related sentence pairs on general text typically score 0.5 with MiniLM embeddings. This metric ensures that if all systems align similarly with their input questions, differences elsewhere reflect insight quality rather than bias in the search process.

The formula is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Q-I\_Alignment} = \frac{1}{|I|} \sum_{i \in I} \text{cosine\_sim}(\text{emb}(Q_i), \text{emb}(T_i))
\end{equation}

where $Q_i$ is the question text, $T_i$ is the insight text, and $\text{emb}(\cdot)$ uses Sentence-BERT embeddings \cite{sbert}.

\textit{Reason-Insight Coherence} measures how close the reason field is to the final insight. This metric is only applicable to the QUIS-style pipeline and LLM baseline because ONLYSTATS uses template-based questions without reason fields.

The formula is:
\begin{equation}
\text{R-I\_Coherence} = \frac{1}{|I|} \sum_{i \in I} \text{cosine\_sim}(\text{emb}(R_i), \text{emb}(T_i))
\end{equation}

where $R_i$ is the reason text for insight $i$. This metric is not applicable to ONLYSTATS.

\section{Experimental Setup}

We use three datasets summarized in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}: Adidas US Sales (Chaudhari, 2022) \cite{adidas}, IBM Employee Attrition (Subhash, 2017) \cite{ibm}, and Online Sales (Verma, 2024) \cite{online}. We use OpenAI's \texttt{gpt-5.4} for the QUIS-Style Pipeline and the LLM Baseline. The original paper used Llama-3-70B-instruct; our model may not produce exact numbers but does not affect comparability since all systems share the same evaluation framework. We set \texttt{beam\_width = 100}, matching the original paper's configuration. For text similarity metrics, we use \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} from Sentence-BERT (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) \cite{sbert}.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Analysis}

Our metrics are interconnected: structural validity enables subspace exploration, which affects subspace quality. This allows us to trace how design choices propagate through the pipeline

\subsubsection{Faithfulness}

All three systems achieve 100\% faithfulness across all datasets, establishing a baseline for comparing other quality dimensions. This confirms that differences in structural validity and exploration quality are not artifacts of numerical errors or hallucination. Schema-aware question generation, statistical selection, and free-form reasoning all maintain correctness when computing aggregations.

\subsubsection{Structural Validity Rate}

SVR shows the largest and most consistent difference across datasets. The structured pipeline achieves 94.0\% average SVR, while the LLM Baseline achieves only 40.0\% (gap of 54 percentage points). ONLYSTATS achieves 90.8\% by constraining its statistics-based selection to valid column types, ensuring breakdown columns match pattern requirements.

The Baseline's core problem is systematic column-type mismatch: it achieves 0\% ATTRIBUTION SVR on Adidas and Online Sales, always selecting numeric columns (e.g., \texttt{Total Sales}, \texttt{MonthlyIncome}) as the breakdown instead of categorical ones. For TREND, it uses categorical breakdowns in 51.5\% of Adidas cases (e.g., \texttt{Region}, \texttt{Product}) instead of temporal columns. The structured pipeline avoids this because schema-aware generation produces cards with explicit, programmatically validated breakdown fields. Notably, the Baseline's prompt explicitly instructs the LLM to use categorical or temporal columns as breakdowns for TREND, ATTRIBUTION, and DISTRIBUTION\_DIFFERENCE patterns, and to avoid numerical columns. However, the LLM fails to follow these instructions consistently, resulting in the observed structural validity issues. This reveals a key limitation: prompt-based constraints cannot reliably enforce structural correctness, while programmatic validation---whether through structured generation or statistical filtering---achieves over 90\% validity across all datasets.

\subsubsection{Pattern Coverage}

All systems miss TREND on Employee Attrition and Online Sales due to limited temporal columns. The Baseline misses ATTRIBUTION on Adidas and Online Sales because its numeric breakdowns are structurally invalid. This limitation stems from dataset characteristics: lack of temporal columns prevents TREND analysis, while the Baseline's structural validity problems prevent ATTRIBUTION generation where categorical breakdowns are required.

\subsubsection{Subspace Exploration}

Subspace Rate reveals where structured approaches show the most advantage. The structured pipeline achieves 84.4\% average subspace rate (range: 79.2\%--87.2\%), compared with 37.4\% for the Baseline (range: 33.3\%--42.7\%) and 62.6\% for ONLYSTATS (range: 60.0\%--65.8\%).

Since the structured pipeline and ONLYSTATS share the same search module, the 21.8-point average gap between them isolates the contribution of semantically grounded questions over exhaustive enumeration. LLM-generated questions appear to focus the search on interpretable subspaces, while statistical selection produces many valid pairs where global patterns are too weak for subspace search to improve. The 47.0-point gap versus the Baseline reflects compounding effects: invalid breakdowns cannot be explored in subspaces at all. ONLYSTATS reaches its highest subspace rate on Online Sales (65.8\%) because exhaustive enumeration produces many valid (B,\,M) pairs on this small 9-column dataset, giving the search more entry points.

Score Uplift is positive on average for all systems (structured: 1.067, Baseline: 0.974, ONLYSTATS: 0.818), measured as the mean pattern strength of subspace insights ($x$). The structured pipeline shows the strongest uplift on Employee Attrition ($x$=1.574) and leads on average across all datasets. On Online Sales, ONLYSTATS achieves the highest Score Uplift ($x$=1.232), while the Baseline achieves $x$=1.048 due to its smaller insight set concentrating on globally stronger patterns. SPR follows a similar trend: ONLYSTATS achieves the highest average (34.7\%) but with moderate variance across datasets (21.3\%--42.9\%), while the structured pipeline achieves 30.1\% with more consistency (27.9\%--32.1\%). The Baseline finds no significant paradoxes across any dataset, confirming that structurally invalid insights cannot produce meaningful subspace patterns.

\subsubsection{Statistical Significance}

Statistical significance is dataset-dependent (structured: 46.4\%, Baseline: 57.6\%, ONLYSTATS: 58.0\% on average). The structured pipeline leads on Adidas (83.4\%) but is less efficient on Employee Attrition (20.0\%), where binary/categorical variables make simple aggregations easier to pass significance tests. This dependency reflects inherent difficulty: temporal datasets with continuous measures favor focused subspace search, while categorical-heavy datasets benefit from exhaustive enumeration.

\subsubsection{Control Metrics}

Q-I Alignment is similar across the structured pipeline (0.540) and Baseline (0.569), confirming that the search module treats both systems comparably---differences come from input quality, not retrieval bias. R-I Coherence slightly favors the structured pipeline on average (0.526 vs 0.514), though the Baseline scores higher on Employee Attrition (0.519 vs 0.468). This reversal is consistent with the Baseline's higher statistical significance on that dataset (55.8\% vs 20.0\%): when Baseline insights happen to be statistically stronger, their text tends to be more specific and better aligned with the reason. Both metrics are not reported for ONLYSTATS (template-based questions only).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Key Findings}

Framework diagnostics reveal three systematic differences. First, programmatic constraints are necessary for structural validity: structured approaches achieve over 90\% SVR while prompt-based reasoning achieves only 40\%, showing that LLMs cannot reliably follow structural constraints through prompting. The constraint itself matters, not the generation method---ONLYSTATS achieves high SVR through statistical filtering, not LLM reasoning. This has direct implications: AutoEDA systems should enforce validity programmatically, not through prompt engineering. Second, semantic grounding improves exploration: the 21.8-point gap between the structured pipeline and ONLYSTATS shows that LLM-generated questions focus search more effectively than exhaustive enumeration. This suggests that effective exploration requires both validity and semantic grounding. Third, structural validity enables meaningful subspace analysis: the baseline's zero significant paradoxes confirm that invalid breakdowns cannot be meaningfully conditioned.

\subsection{Limitations}

We use \texttt{gpt-5.4} instead of the original Llama-3-70B-instruct, so exact numbers may differ. Our embedding metrics depend on \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}. With only three datasets, metric differences should be read as observations rather than statistical conclusions. Nevertheless, the 54-point SVR gap and 47-point Subspace Rate gap are consistent across datasets.

\section{Conclusion}

We present a reproducible automatic evaluation framework for AutoEDA systems with 9 metrics covering correctness, structural validity, statistical quality, and subspace exploration. Across three datasets, the framework reveals three outstanding findings. First, the LLM Baseline has a systematic structural validity problem (37.0\%--45.3\% SVR) caused by column-type mismatches, while the structured pipeline achieves 86.8\%--99.0\% across datasets. Second, semantically grounded questions enable better subspace discovery (84.4\% Subspace Rate vs 37.4\%) with stronger and more consistent patterns (SPR 30.1\%, Score Uplift $x$=1.067). Third, structural validity is a prerequisite for meaningful subspace analysis: systems with invalid breakdowns produce zero significant paradoxes, while structured approaches discover them in two of three datasets. These findings have two practical implications: LLM-based EDA systems should validate breakdown column types programmatically, not through prompting; and insight search engines benefit from semantically grounded input over exhaustive enumeration alone. Our main contribution is the evaluation framework itself, which is a reusable tool for comparing any AutoEDA system in a consistent and reproducible way.
